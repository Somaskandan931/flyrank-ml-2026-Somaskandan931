WHICH PAGE FIRST? A TWO-SIGNAL PLAYBOOK FOR CONTENT REVIEW PRIORITY
FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Research Paper

Key figures: 176,738 content-client pairs scored | 0.882 honest ROC AUC
(client-held-out) | 9.7% of the queue carries a model score | 60,624 rows
the playbook refuses to touch


================================================================
1. ABSTRACT
================================================================
Content teams cannot manually review every page in a large portfolio, so
the question this paper asks is: given one month of search and engagement
data, which pages should a human review first, and why? The method
rebuilds a whole-month CTR-versus-position-benchmark baseline that needs
no model, trains a Random Forest on two-week-early features to predict
two-week-late click volume, validates it under a client-grouped holdout
so no client's pages appear on both sides of the split, and audits the
result for label leakage and base-rate distortion before trusting it.
Crossing the baseline and the honest model score produces six named
archetypes — from CONFIRMED_PRIORITY, where both signals agree, down to
LOW_VOLUME_NOISE, which the playbook excludes outright. On the March 2026
slice, the honest model reaches a Precision@50 of 1.0 and a ROC AUC of
0.882 against a 0.372 base rate, while a naive random split would have
overstated generalization by letting 32 clients leak across train and
test. The result is a ranked queue of 176,738 pages with an action label
and a plain-language reason for each one, not a black-box score.


================================================================
2. INTRODUCTION
================================================================
A content team managing dozens of clients and tens of thousands of pages
cannot read every analytics dashboard by hand. The practical decision
this work supports is narrower than "will refreshing this page improve
rankings" — it is where should a reviewer's limited hour go first this
week. Getting that wrong has a real cost in both directions: a false
positive wastes a reviewer's time on a page that was already fine, and a
false negative lets a genuinely declining page keep losing visibility
while nobody looks at it.

Two lanes were considered during exploration on a 30,000-page anonymized
starter sample spanning 32 clients, where 54% of pages already carried a
declining trend label. That sample confirmed the underlying problem was
worth solving, but it was set aside once the full warehouse became
available, because the warehouse's raw daily GSC/GA4 rows support a
genuinely held-out future window rather than a single static label. The
final approach combines two independent lanes instead of picking one: a
CTR/engagement opportunity score that needs no model and covers every
page, and a refresh/growth model that adds a second, forward-looking
opinion wherever there is enough held-out evidence to trust it.


================================================================
3. DATA
================================================================
The analysis uses fact_content_daily_performance from the FlyRank ML
internship warehouse, filtered to March 2026. One row is one (content,
client, date) combination. The month was split into a features window
and a label window with no shared dates:

  - Early window — March 1-15: source of every feature.
  - Late window  — March 16-31: source of the label only.

Coverage of March 2026, whole-month scan:

  Slice                          Rows          Share of total
  ------------------------------ ------------- --------------
  Total daily rows               9,841,378     100%
  Rows with GSC data available   3,611,061     36.7%
  Rows with GA4 data available     413,966      4.2%

This is an unbalanced panel — coverage is not uniform across clients or
across the month — so GA4-derived features (sessions, scroll events) are
directionally weaker or missing wherever a row is GSC-only. The final
development month (June 2026) was never touched; it is the warehouse's
sealed test window and stays out of scope for this paper. No client
names, domains, URLs, or raw exports appear anywhere in this paper or
its outputs, consistent with the internship's public-data rule.

Fields used:
  Features (early window only): gsc_impressions_early, gsc_clicks_early,
  gsc_avg_position_early, ga4_sessions_early, scroll_events_early.
  Label: gsc_clicks_late, converted to a binary "above this month's
  median" flag.
  Excluded: whole-month gsc_avg_position (would blend in post-decision
  data) and any late-window engagement metric (measured in the same
  period as the label).


================================================================
4. METHODOLOGY
================================================================

--- Step 1 — The baseline: CTR Recovery Priority ---
For every (content, client) pair, actual CTR is compared to the average
CTR of other content in the same position band. When actual CTR falls
below that band's benchmark, the shortfall — multiplied by impression
volume — becomes the score, so a large gap on a high-traffic page
outranks the same gap on a low-traffic page. No future window and no
product flag enters this calculation; it uses only contemporaneous GSC
counts.

CTR by position band, whole-month March 2026 (confirms CTR falls as
position worsens):

  Position band   Impressions      CTR
  --------------- ---------------- ------
  1-3             54,028,594       0.38%
  4-10            137,830,113      0.32%
  11-20           29,386,006       0.31%
  21-50           55,944,412       0.14%
  51+             3,468,464        0.04%

The companion check — whether higher-impression pages under-convert
more — came back mixed rather than confirmed: the under-100-impression
tier showed a higher average CTR than the 100-999 tier, which is the
signature of a small denominator inflating a ratio, not a real pattern.
That is exactly why the baseline floors out any row under 50 monthly
impressions before it ever reaches a human.

--- Step 2 — The model, and why a random split lies ---
A Random Forest is trained on the five early-window features to predict
label_high_clicks_late. Reporting this honestly required comparing two
splits on the identical data, model, and features:

  Split                    Clients shared  Precision@50  ROC AUC  Avg. precision
  ------------------------ --------------- ------------- -------- ---------------
  Naive random (no group.) 32              1.000         0.877    0.846
  Client-grouped (honest)  0               1.000         0.882    0.832

The gap is smaller than a worst case would suggest here, but the naive
split let 32 of 33 clients place pages on both sides of train/test — a
real risk of the model partly memorizing client-level character rather
than generalizing. Only the grouped number is reported anywhere
downstream.

--- Step 3 — Leakage audit on the shipped features ---
Train-with / train-without test on the honest split, Random Forest ROC
AUC:

  Feature set                        ROC AUC   Drop vs. full
  ----------------------------------- --------- --------------
  All five features                   0.882     —
  Without gsc_impressions_early       0.843     -0.039
  Without gsc_clicks_early            0.870     -0.012
  Without gsc_avg_position_early      0.874     -0.008
  Without ga4_sessions_early          0.881     -0.000
  Without scroll_events_early         0.883     +0.001

No single feature carries a near-1.0 score on its own, and every feature
comes from a time window that ends before the label window begins — the
confession pattern a genuinely leaked feature would show is absent here.

--- Step 4 — Combining both signals into the queue ---
The two signals are crossed into six archetypes. The model's score is
applied only to rows that actually fell in the client-grouped holdout —
applying it to its own training rows would reuse memorized information
as if it were a prediction, which is exactly the mistake the split audit
exists to catch.


================================================================
5. RESULTS
================================================================
Five model families were compared on the same honest, client-grouped
split before Random Forest was selected for the queue:

  Method                              Precision@50  ROC AUC  Avg. precision
  ----------------------------------- ------------- -------- ---------------
  Baseline CTR-recovery only (Wk 4)   0.94           0.567    0.554
  Logistic regression                 1.00           0.871    0.825
  Decision tree                       0.94           0.877    0.817
  Random Forest (shipped)             1.00           0.882    0.832
  Gradient boosting                   1.00           0.884    0.834

Gradient boosting edges out Random Forest by a hair on this split;
Random Forest was carried forward into the playbook for its more stable
behavior across the leakage audit's feature-removal tests. The baseline
alone — no model, no held-out data required — already clears a 0.94
Precision@50 against a 0.372 base rate, which is why it remains the
layer that covers every page rather than only the 9.7% with a model
score.

The ranked queue, by archetype:

  Archetype                       Count     Share
  -------------------------------- --------- -------
  BASELINE_ONLY_NO_MODEL_SCORE     73,015    41.3%
  LOW_VOLUME_NOISE                 60,624    34.3%
  HEALTHY_OR_MONITOR               30,212    17.1%
  BASELINE_ONLY_LOW_CONFIDENCE      6,469     3.7%
  CONFIRMED_PRIORITY                3,501     2.0%
  MODEL_ONLY_WATCH                  2,917     1.7%


================================================================
6. LIMITATIONS & HONEST FRAMING
================================================================
What this paper CAN say: the model's score is an observed, out-of-sample
ranking signal on one month of one warehouse slice. It is decision
support for where a reviewer should look first — not proof that
reviewing a page will increase its clicks, and not a claim about
Google's ranking algorithm.

What it CANNOT say: that any specific page will grow, that a title/meta
edit causes a click increase, or that this ranking holds outside March
2026 without re-validation.

  - Coverage. Only 36.7% of March rows have GSC data and 4.2% have GA4
    data; roughly a third of clients have little or no usable history
    and fall back to baseline-only treatment entirely.
  - One month, one split. The model was trained and honestly evaluated
    on a single early/late split inside March 2026. It says nothing
    about April, about seasonality, or about a client whose reporting
    starts later.
  - The label is a relative proxy. "High clicks late" means late-window
    clicks exceeded this dataset's own median — a portfolio-relative
    definition, not an external growth or revenue outcome.
  - Small-sample noise. 44,242 baseline-flagged rows sit under 50
    monthly impressions, where a handful of clicks can swing a CTR
    ratio dramatically; this is exactly why LOW_VOLUME_NOISE is
    excluded outright rather than merely down-weighted.
  - Confidence, not certainty. A model score is an honest probability
    from a grouped holdout, not a guarantee — the only numbers that
    describe how good the ranking actually is are the Precision@50,
    ROC AUC, and base rate reported above, and they should be
    re-checked whenever the portfolio or month changes.


================================================================
7. RANKED RECOMMENDATIONS
================================================================
Six archetypes, each with one action and one reason a human can check in
seconds. Priority rank follows the order below; LOW_VOLUME_NOISE never
enters the ranking at all.

CONFIRMED PRIORITY — Review title & meta (do first)
  Baseline flags the CTR gap AND the held-out model independently scores
  it >=0.5. Two signals agree, and the fix is cheap relative to the
  impression volume already flowing to the page. 3,501 pages, 2.0%.

MODEL-ONLY WATCH — Investigate momentum
  The page isn't broken by today's CTR standard, but the model sees
  rising momentum. Lower urgency, higher upside — worth expanding before
  it plateaus. 2,917 pages, 1.7%.

BASELINE-ONLY, NO MODEL SCORE — Review title & meta (baseline confidence)
  The baseline flags it, but the page fell outside the held-out sample,
  so there's no second opinion. Still actionable — kept separate from
  Confirmed Priority on purpose, because it's a single-signal call.
  73,015 pages, 41.3%.

BASELINE-ONLY, LOW CONFIDENCE — Low-priority review
  The baseline flags it, but the held-out model disagrees. The two
  signals disagree — worth a look, not worth doing first. 6,469 pages,
  3.7%.

HEALTHY OR MONITOR — Monitor
  Neither signal flags the page. No action, revisit next cycle. 30,212
  pages, 17.1%.

LOW-VOLUME NOISE — Exclude (insufficient data)
  Fewer than 50 monthly impressions. A handful of impressions can fake a
  large relative CTR gap; this tier is never acted on regardless of
  score. 60,624 pages, 34.3%.

What a human checks before acting:
Before touching any Confirmed Priority, Baseline-Only, or Model-Only
Watch row: is a title/meta change already in flight that this month's
average hasn't caught up to yet? Is the impression volume real search
intent, or a low-relevance query mix inflating the count? Is the page
seasonal, legal, medical, or otherwise brand-sensitive, where a template
edit needs a specialist instead?

The no-go list:
The queue never auto-publishes or auto-applies anything — every row is a
recommendation to review, not an edit to execute. model_score is never
treated as ground truth on its own, a FlyRank-style composite or
workflow label never feeds back in as a re-scoring input, and the
pipeline is never silently extended to a client or month it wasn't
re-validated on.

Monitoring — when this playbook goes stale (reference values, March
2026):

  Signal                            Reference value   Retrain if
  ---------------------------------- ----------------- --------------------------
  Precision@50 (honest split)        1.000             Falls >20% relative
  Label base rate                    0.372             Drifts >10 points
  Benchmark CTR (any position band)  see table above   Moves >20% relative
  Portfolio coverage (GSC available) 36.7%             Shifts sharply MoM

8. REPRODUCIBILITY
================================================================
Every step above traces to a notebook committed under work/notebooks/ in
the project repository: a research-question framing pass, an ML-task
framing pass, a data contract with a deliberately injected and then
removed label leak, a baseline CTR-recovery score with a top-10 manual
review, a first honest model with a naive-vs-grouped leakage
demonstration, a validation and research-claim audit against FlyRank's
own published findings, and the action-playbook notebook that produced
the queue above. The full ranked queue (w07_action_queue.csv, 176,738
rows) is regenerated on every run and intentionally excluded from
version control; the aggregate counts and monitoring reference values in
w07_playbook_metrics.json are committed and are the receipt every number
on this page traces back to.
